You forgot your pen, and the person next to you has two. You want to ask, but you miss the moment while you’re working out how to say it. That happens to everyone.

This guide shows **how to ask permission in Japanese** for the people you actually talk to: a friend, a teacher, a coworker, your boss. You’ll learn one basic pattern, how to dial it up or down, when the very polite させていただけますか (*sasete itadakemasu ka*) really fits, and how to understand the answer.

## The basic pattern: て-form + もいいですか

Most everyday ways of asking permission are built on one pattern: put the verb in its て-form (*te*-form), then add もいいですか (*mo ii desu ka*). Literally, it means “Is it all right even if I do this?”

| Dictionary form | て-form | Asking permission |
|---|---|---|
| {入|はい}る (*hairu*, to enter) | {入|はい}って (*haitte*) | {入|はい}ってもいいですか。(*Haitte mo ii desu ka?*) |
| {使|つか}う (*tsukau*, to use) | {使|つか}って (*tsukatte*) | {使|つか}ってもいいですか。(*Tsukatte mo ii desu ka?*) |
| {開|あ}ける (*akeru*, to open) | {開|あ}けて (*akete*) | {開|あ}けてもいいですか。(*Akete mo ii desu ka?*) |

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">これ、{使|つか}って<mark>もいいですか</mark>。</span><span class="ro">Kore, tsukatte mo ii desu ka?</span><span class="tr">May I use this?</span></div>

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">{窓|まど}を{開|あ}けて<mark>もいいですか</mark>。</span><span class="ro">Mado o akete mo ii desu ka?</span><span class="tr">May I open the window?</span></div>

You’ll also hear the shorter 〜ていいですか, without も (*Tsukatte ii desu ka?*). It asks for permission in the same way, and it’s very common in everyday conversation.

If you only learn one phrase from this guide, make it 〜てもいいですか. It’s polite enough for teachers, coworkers, and shop staff, and it won’t sound rude.

## Choosing by who you’re asking

The same request can sound casual or very formal. Here are the steps, from friends at the bottom to your boss or a customer at the top.

| Phrase | Romaji | How polite | Who you might say it to |
|---|---|---|---|
| {使|つか}っていい? | *Tsukatte ii?* | Casual | Friends, family |
| {使|つか}ってもいいですか。 | *Tsukatte mo ii desu ka?* | Polite | Teachers, coworkers, shop staff. Use this when you’re not sure |
| {使|つか}ってもかまいませんか。 | *Tsukatte mo kamaimasen ka?* | A little more careful | When you want to check it’s no trouble |
| {使|つか}ってもよろしいですか。 | *Tsukatte mo yoroshii desu ka?* | Very polite | Your boss, customers, people much older than you |
| {使|つか}ってもよろしいでしょうか。 | *Tsukatte mo yoroshii deshō ka?* | Even more formal | Formal situations, important requests |
| {使|つか}わせていただけますか。 | *Tsukawasete itadakemasu ka?* | Most polite | When you need their permission and you benefit (next section) |

A few notes on the words in the middle:

- かまいません (*kamaimasen*) comes from かまわない (*kamawanai*), which means “no problem” or “I don’t mind.” So {使|つか}ってもかまいませんか asks, “Would it be any trouble if I used it?”
- よろしい (*yoroshii*) is a more polite way of saying いい (*ii*, “good, okay”). Swapping いい for よろしい instantly makes your question more formal.

<div class="analogy"><span class="lbl">THINK OF IT LIKE THIS</span><p>Think of these phrases as outfits. You’re the same person whether you meet a friend for coffee or go to a job interview, but you change clothes for the occasion. The request stays the same, and only the “outfit” of the words changes. This is just a way to picture it, not a rule that covers every situation.</p></div>

## When to use 〜(さ)せていただけますか

〜(さ)せていただけますか (*-(sa)sete itadakemasu ka*) is the most polite step, and it’s easy to overuse. A report on polite language published by Japan’s Agency for Cultural Affairs ({敬語|けいご}の{指針|ししん}, *Keigo no shishin*) explains when it fits. The form fits best when two things are true:

1. You’re doing something **with the other person’s permission**.
2. You **benefit** from being allowed to do it.

Borrowing someone’s book to copy a page is a perfect fit. You need their OK, and the copy helps you.

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">コピーを{取|と}らせて<mark>いただけますか</mark>。</span><span class="ro">Kopī o torasete itadakemasu ka?</span><span class="tr">Would you allow me to make a copy?</span></div>

When the conditions don’t really apply, the report treats the phrase as less fitting, and it can come across as wordy or out of place. Save it for moments that truly match.

To make the form, you need the causative (〜せる / 〜させる, *-seru / -saseru*) form of the verb. There are three rules:

- **Most verbs (u-verbs):** change the last sound to the “a” row and add せる. {使|つか}う → {使|つか}わせて (*tsukawasete*), {取|と}る → {取|と}らせて (*torasete*), {読|よ}む → {読|よ}ませて (*yomasete*).
- **る-verbs:** replace る with させる. {開|あ}ける → {開|あ}けさせて (*akesasete*).
- **Irregular:** する → させて (*sasete*), {来|く}る → {来|こ}させて (*kosasete*).

For u-verbs, the standard form has no extra さ: {読|よ}ませて (*yomasete*), not {読|よ}まさせて (*yomasasete*).

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">{窓|まど}を{開|あ}けさせて<mark>いただけますか</mark>。</span><span class="ro">Mado o akesasete itadakemasu ka?</span><span class="tr">Would you allow me to open the window?</span></div>

## The look-alike: 〜てもらっていいですか asks someone else to do it

Here’s a phrase that sounds almost the same but points the other way.

![Two scenes. On the left, labeled kaite mo ii desu ka, a person reaches toward a sheet of paper: I write. On the right, labeled kaite moratte ii desu ka, the other person reaches toward the paper: You write.](/illustrations/permission-in-japanese/fig-1.webp)

<div class="ex-pair">
<div class="example"><span class="lbl">I DO IT</span><span class="jpx">{書|か}いて<mark>もいいですか</mark>。</span><span class="ro">Kaite mo ii desu ka?</span><span class="tr">May I write?</span></div>
<div class="example"><span class="lbl">YOU DO IT</span><span class="jpx">{書|か}いて<mark>もらっていいですか</mark>。</span><span class="ro">Kaite moratte ii desu ka?</span><span class="tr">Could you write it (for me)?</span></div>
</div>

With もらう (*morau*, “to receive”) added, the question isn’t about something *you* will do. It asks whether the other person is willing to do it. You’ll often hear it at offices and shops:

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">ここにお{名前|なまえ}とご{住所|じゅうしょ}を{書|か}いて<mark>もらっていいですか</mark>。</span><span class="ro">Koko ni onamae to gojūsho o kaite moratte ii desu ka?</span><span class="tr">Could you write your name and address here?</span></div>

So if a clerk says this to you, they aren’t asking for your permission. They’re asking you to do something. The Japan Foundation article that describes this phrase also notes that some people find it a little pushy, so keep that in mind before using it yourself.

## How people answer

When the answer is yes, you’ll usually hear one of these:

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">はい、どうぞ。</span><span class="ro">Hai, dōzo.</span><span class="tr">Sure, go ahead.</span></div>

You might also hear いいですよ (*Ii desu yo*, “That’s fine”) or もちろん (*Mochiron*, “Of course”).

A “no” is often softer than you expect. Instead of saying no directly, people may say ちょっと (*chotto*, “a little”) and let the sentence trail off.

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">すみません、ちょっと…。</span><span class="ro">Sumimasen, chotto…</span><span class="tr">Sorry, that’s a bit… (difficult).</span></div>

When ちょっと is followed by a negative, it means something isn’t easy to do. In conversation, people often leave the negative part unsaid, so ちょっと… on its own works as a gentle no. If you hear it, take it as a polite refusal and don’t push.
