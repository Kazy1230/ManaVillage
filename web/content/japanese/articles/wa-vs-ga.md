In English translations, <b>は</b> and <b>が</b> usually just disappear, because English has no word for either. So when you study **wa vs ga**, you end up guessing. If you’ve ever stared at a sentence and flipped a mental coin between the two, you’re not alone.

This guide gives you one simple picture to decide with, shows both particles in real conversations, and ends with a short quiz so you can check yourself.

<div class="points"><span class="lbl">IN 30 SECONDS</span>

- <b>は</b> sets the **topic**: what the sentence is about.
- <b>が</b> points at **one thing**: something new you just noticed, or the one you picked.
- When a question word like {誰|だれ} (who) is the **subject**, use <b>が</b>.

</div>

![A stage with a striped kabuki curtain. A spotlight shines on one stick figure, labeled が = this one!, while two gray figures stand on the stage labeled は = the stage.](/illustrations/wa-vs-ga/core.webp)

## The stage and the spotlight

A handy way to picture these two particles is a theater, and we’ll use the same picture all the way through this guide.

<div class="analogy"><span class="lbl">THINK OF IT LIKE THIS</span><p>Imagine a stage. <b>は</b> turns on the stage lights: “OK, now we’re talking about <em>this</em>.” Everyone in the audience knows what the scene is about. <b>が</b> is a spotlight that swings onto one person: “It’s <em>this one</em>.” This is just a picture to help you remember. It doesn’t cover every use of the two particles.</p></div>

Keep the picture in mind, and let’s look at each particle on its own.

## は: setting the stage

Use <b>は</b> to say what you’re talking about. In a self-introduction, you are the topic, so you put yourself on the stage first.

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">{私|わたし}<mark>は</mark>{学生|がくせい}です。</span><span class="ro">Watashi wa gakusei desu.</span><span class="tr">I’m a student. (As for me, I’m a student.)</span></div>

Once the stage is set, the rest of the sentence just says something about it. That takes some pressure off. If you’ve ever frozen halfway through introducing yourself, starting with {私|わたし}は gives you a moment to think about what comes next.

<b>は</b> is also used for general facts and the nature of things.

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">{空|そら}<mark>は</mark>{青|あお}い。</span><span class="ro">Sora wa aoi.</span><span class="tr">The sky is blue. (A general fact about the sky.)</span></div>

## が: the spotlight

Use <b>が</b> when you point at one thing. Two everyday cases come up again and again.

### Something you just noticed

When something new appears and you report it, <b>が</b> is the natural choice. You weren’t talking about the bus before. It just showed up, and the spotlight swings onto it.

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">あ、バス<mark>が</mark>{来|き}た。</span><span class="ro">A, basu ga kita.</span><span class="tr">Oh, here comes the bus.</span></div>

### The one you picked

When you choose one thing out of several, <b>が</b> marks your choice, in both the question and the answer.

<div class="ex-pair">
<div class="example"><span class="lbl">QUESTION</span><span class="jpx">どれ<mark>が</mark>いいですか。</span><span class="ro">Dore ga ii desu ka?</span><span class="tr">Which one would you like?</span></div>
<div class="example"><span class="lbl">ANSWER</span><span class="jpx">これ<mark>が</mark>いいです。</span><span class="ro">Kore ga ii desu.</span><span class="tr">I’d like this one.</span></div>
</div>

### Same words, different scenes

Here is where the difference really shows. The same two words, “no money,” take a different particle depending on the scene.

**Scene 1.** You finish dinner at a restaurant, open your wallet, and… it’s empty. You turn to your friend:

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">あ…お{金|かね}<mark>が</mark>ないんです。</span><span class="ro">A… okane ga nai n desu.</span><span class="tr">Oh no… I don’t have any money.</span></div>

You just discovered it, and you’re reporting it. That’s the spotlight, so you use <b>が</b>.

**Scene 2.** A teenager asks a parent, “お{金|かね}{貸|か}して” (*Okane kashite*, “Lend me some money”). Money is already the topic, because the teenager just brought it up. The parent answers:

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">お{金|かね}<mark>は</mark>ないの。</span><span class="ro">Okane wa nai no.</span><span class="tr">I don’t have money (to lend you).</span></div>

The stage is already set (“money”), and the parent says something about it. So you use <b>は</b>.

## When the question word is the subject, use が

Question words such as {誰|だれ} (who), {何|なに} (what), and どれ (which one) ask about something you don’t know yet. When one of them is the **subject** of the sentence, it takes <b>が</b>, and so does the answer.

<div class="ex-pair">
<div class="example"><span class="lbl">QUESTION</span><span class="jpx">{誰|だれ}<mark>が</mark>{来|き}ましたか。</span><span class="ro">Dare ga kimashita ka?</span><span class="tr">Who came?</span></div>
<div class="example"><span class="lbl">ANSWER</span><span class="jpx">{田中|たなか}さん<mark>が</mark>{来|き}ました。</span><span class="ro">Tanaka-san ga kimashita.</span><span class="tr">Tanaka came.</span></div>
</div>

In our picture, the person you’re asking about isn’t on the stage yet. You can’t set the stage with someone you don’t know, so you send the spotlight looking for them instead.

<div class="ex-pair">
<div class="example ng"><span class="lbl">NG</span><span class="jpx">{誰|だれ}は{来|き}ましたか。</span><span class="ro">Dare wa kimashita ka?</span><span class="tr">(intended: Who came?)</span><span class="why">{誰|だれ} asks about someone unknown, so it can’t be the topic.</span></div>
<div class="example ok"><span class="lbl">OK</span><span class="jpx">{誰|だれ}<mark>が</mark>{来|き}ましたか。</span><span class="ro">Dare ga kimashita ka?</span><span class="tr">Who came?</span></div>
</div>

One note on scope: this rule is about question words used as the subject. In questions like {何|なに}を{食|た}べますか (*Nani o tabemasu ka?*, “What will you eat?”), the question word takes a different particle, and は vs が isn’t the issue.

## は and が in one sentence

The two particles often appear together: <b>は</b> sets the stage, and <b>が</b> points at something on it.

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">{私|わたし}<mark>は</mark>{日本語|にほんご}<mark>が</mark>{好|す}きです。</span><span class="ro">Watashi wa nihongo ga suki desu.</span><span class="tr">I like Japanese.</span></div>

The stage is “me,” and the spotlight is on what I like. With words like {好|す}き (like), the thing you like is marked with <b>が</b>.

A famous example works the same way:

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">{象|ぞう}<mark>は</mark>{鼻|はな}<mark>が</mark>{長|なが}い。</span><span class="ro">Zō wa hana ga nagai.</span><span class="tr">Elephants have long trunks. (As for elephants, the trunk is long.)</span></div>

The whole elephant is on the stage, and the spotlight picks out one part of it, the trunk.

## は for contrast

<b>は</b> can also set up two stages side by side to compare them.

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">{魚|さかな}<mark>は</mark>{食|た}べますが、{肉|にく}<mark>は</mark>ちょっと…。</span><span class="ro">Sakana wa tabemasu ga, niku wa chotto…</span><span class="tr">I eat fish, but meat… not really.</span></div>

Notice something here: in this sentence, <b>は</b> isn’t replacing <b>が</b>. It sits where を would normally go ({魚|さかな}を{食|た}べます, “I eat fish”). <b>は</b> can take the place of more than one particle when you want to make something the topic.

## A common slip that isn’t quite wrong

Some sentences with <b>が</b> are grammatically fine but sound odd in the wrong scene. In a self-introduction, for example:

<div class="example"><span class="lbl">EXAMPLE</span><span class="jpx">{私|わたし}が{学生|がくせい}です。</span><span class="ro">Watashi ga gakusei desu.</span><span class="tr">*I’m* the one who’s the student.</span></div>

This isn’t a mistake. It’s the perfect answer if someone asks, “Which of you is the student?” But when you’re just introducing yourself, it sounds like you’re answering a question nobody asked. Use {私|わたし}は there instead.

## は and が side by side

| | は (wa) | が (ga) |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Sets the topic: what you’re talking about | Points at one thing |
| When you use it | Self-introductions, general facts, comparing two things | Something you just noticed, the one you picked, a question word as the subject |
| Example | {私|わたし}は{学生|がくせい}です。 | あ、バスが{来|き}た。 |
| In the picture | The stage lights | The spotlight |

## Quick quiz

Fill in each blank with <b>は</b> or <b>が</b>. The scene is given in parentheses.

1. (Introducing yourself to a new classmate) はじめまして。{私|わたし}＿{田中|たなか}です。
2. (Asking about a trip) {誰|だれ}＿{行|い}きますか。
3. (Looking out the window) あ、{雨|あめ}＿{降|ふ}ってきた。
4. (Talking about yourself) {私|わたし}＿{猫|ねこ}＿{好|す}きです。
5. (Telling a host what you drink) コーヒー＿{飲|の}みますが、{紅茶|こうちゃ}＿{飲|の}みません。

**Answers**

1. <b>は</b>. *Hajimemashite. Watashi wa Tanaka desu.* You’re setting the stage: “As for me…”
2. <b>が</b>. *Dare ga ikimasu ka?* The question word is the subject.
3. <b>が</b>. *A, ame ga futte kita.* You just noticed the rain and you’re reporting it.
4. <b>は</b>, then <b>が</b>. *Watashi wa neko ga suki desu.* “Me” is the stage, and cats are what you like.
5. <b>は</b>, <b>は</b>. *Kōhī wa nomimasu ga, kōcha wa nomimasen.* Two things are compared. Like the fish example, <b>は</b> sits where を would go.
